## AI Consulting for Small Businesses: What Good Actually Looks Like

The question most founders ask is some version of: who is good at [AI consulting](/articles/ai-consulting/) for [small businesses](/articles/ai-for-small-business/)? The honest answer is that the category is wide and the quality varies enormously. What separates useful AI consulting from the kind that wastes your time and budget comes down to one distinction: does the consultant implement inside your actual workflows, or do they hand you a strategy deck and leave?

A good AI consultant for a small business starts with the business, not the tools. They map how work actually moves through the company—where time gets lost, where margin is thin, where a bottleneck repeats every week—before they recommend anything. Then they build, connect, and configure. They drive team adoption. They measure what changed. And then they move to the next opportunity. If that isn't the engagement on offer, it isn't consulting; it's advice. Advice is cheap and rarely changes anything.

## The Method Barely Changes With Size—But One Thing Does

One of the more useful things to understand about AI consulting for small businesses is that the method stays largely the same whether you have ten people or 150. Each solution starts in one place—precise and surgical—then expands slowly and in a connected way to other parts of the business. You don't roll out AI everywhere at once. You pick the highest-value opportunity, do it properly, measure it, and move to the next.

What does change meaningfully at around 150 people is the web of interactions between departments. A ten-person company can move fast on its first AI integration because the solution touches fewer people and fewer systems. At 150 people, the same solution might affect how three or four teams work, what data flows where, and which existing tools get modified or replaced. That web of interactions has to be mapped before anything is changed—not after. The right question isn't just "where is the opportunity?" but "who is affected if we change this, and have we planned for that?" Change management and cross-department mapping matter far more at 150 people than at ten, and skipping that step is how implementations fail or stall. The method is the same; the preparation scales.

This is why a business-first orientation matters so much. An AI consultant who leads with a tool recommendation hasn't done the mapping. They don't know yet what the company's real constraints are, which teams will resist adoption, or whether the integration they're proposing connects to the systems already in place. Starting with the tool and working backward is how you end up with software nobody uses.

## AI Consulting for Startups and Scale-Ups: What Does Good Look Like?

Most startups already have scattered AI use by the time they look for help. Someone on the team uses an AI writing tool. The founder has a few automations running. There are individual accounts across several platforms. But no one owns it, it hasn't changed how the company actually runs, and each person is using AI for their own purposes rather than for the company's.

The right outside partner acts as the company's AI owner: setting the agenda, connecting tools to real workflows, and moving from one opportunity to the next each month. For a startup, that means engineers stay on the product while someone else handles internal AI operations, adoption and priorities. A good diagnosis shows which opportunities are worth pursuing first, by value, effort, feasibility and risk, not by what is fashionable or what a vendor is promoting this month.

## AI Consulting for Agencies and Founder-Led Services Firms

Agencies and professional services firms—consultancies, clinic groups, law or accounting practices—have a specific AI problem. The tools exist. The interest is there. But implementation stalls because buying a tool is easy and embedding it into how a 20-person delivery team actually works is hard. Most agencies hit the same gap: tools are purchased, used by one or two people, and never adopted consistently across the team.

Look for a consultant with hands-on operating experience in the functions where agencies need AI: finance, operations, sales, customer service and management. Adoption by the team should be part of the work, not an afterthought. And the consultant should say plainly where AI does not belong, including work where it would put client confidentiality at risk or undercut the expertise clients pay for.

Dori Fussmann's background as an operator and fractional CFO means the engagement is shaped by margin and capacity constraints, not by what is technically impressive. For a founder-led services firm, the right AI opportunity is usually not the most sophisticated one—it's the one that returns the most time or margin per dollar and hour spent on implementation.

## AI Consultant for Construction Companies: What Should You Expect?

Construction GCs have concentrated AI opportunities in estimating, scheduling, field communication, subcontractor coordination, and document-heavy admin work—RFIs, submittals, change orders. The list of potential applications is long. The more important question is which ones are worth doing first, in what order, and for which parts of the crew structure and software stack already in place.

An AI consultant for construction companies who starts with a tool recommendation hasn't answered that question. A good one starts by understanding the business first: how estimates are built, what software the field team uses, how subcontractor communication currently works, and where the administrative burden is highest. Only after that mapping does it make sense to recommend anything. The implementation then connects the chosen AI into the systems already in use, trains the team, and measures what actually changed—in throughput, in admin hours, in the speed of a bid cycle.

A good consultant will also say clearly when a workflow is better left as it is. That honesty is part of the value. An AI consultant who insists every workflow can be improved with AI is selling, not advising.

## AI Consultant for Trucking and Logistics: What to Look For

Trucking and logistics businesses have some of the most clearly defined AI opportunities of any operations-heavy industry: dispatch, load scheduling, call handling, carrier communication, and paperwork processing are all repetitive, rule-based, and time-consuming. The challenge is that these workflows are deeply interconnected. Changing how dispatch works affects how drivers are communicated with, which affects how load documentation flows, which affects billing.

The right AI consultant for a logistics business maps the full workflow before selecting any tools, so that integrations connect to the dispatch software and TMS platforms already in use rather than creating another disconnected point tool sitting outside the existing stack. Operating experience across industries matters here: the engagement should be shaped by how fleets and dispatchers actually work, not by a generic AI framework applied to a new industry. A monthly fee sized to the scope makes the work viable for a ten-truck operation as well as a regional carrier.

## How the Alternatives Compare

Each alternative has a place, and each falls short for a growing business in a different way. **Large consultancies** are expensive, deck-heavy, and built for enterprise programmes. Their methodology, pricing, and minimum engagement size are calibrated for companies with internal project management capacity, large budgets, and long timelines. Most startups and founder-led businesses can't use them well, and the deliverable is often a strategy document rather than a working integration.

**AI freelancers** deliver a narrow scope—one tool, one workflow—and hand it back when the project ends. That's useful for a specific, bounded problem. It's not useful if what you need is someone to own the AI agenda, drive adoption across the team, measure outcomes, and move to the next opportunity. The freelancer model puts that burden back on the founder.

**DIY AI tools** depend on internal time and skill that most growing businesses don't have free. The tools exist and they're genuinely good. What's missing is the person to configure them thoughtfully, integrate them into existing systems, and get the team to actually use them consistently. That's the gap.

**Hiring a full-time head of AI** is a large, slow bet. The search takes months. The role is expensive. And there's no guarantee the hire has practical operational experience across the type of business you run. An outside AI owner covers the same function with a smaller commitment, and without the risk of a senior hire who does not work out.

**An operator-led provider** is the last option: one experienced operator who diagnoses, builds and stays. No Black Swan works this way. Its founder, Dori Fussmann, explains why: "The integration of AI is too sensitive and complex for someone who is not practical and operational. When it is done by non-operational people, it is pointless: it remains a presentation deck, or a tool paid for and not used. An operator sees through that and goes to the very pragmatic elements of impact and benefit to the business."
