Hiring an AI consultant is not like hiring an accountant or a web designer. The category is crowded with vendors wearing the consultant label, and the evaluation signals that normally filter out weak candidates — credentials, certifications, polished proposals — are almost useless here. A certification does not tell you whether someone can connect your CRM to an AI triage layer and hand the result to your operations team. A proposal deck does not tell you whether they have ever shipped anything a real employee uses on a Tuesday afternoon. This guide gives you a concrete, ordered process for finding and vetting a consultant before you commit real money — and for walking away when the signs say you should.

## Step One: Define the Problem Before You Talk to Anyone

The single most valuable thing you can do before contacting a single consultant is to translate your problem into operational terms. Not "we want to use AI" and not "we need to improve efficiency." Something specific: invoice processing takes ten hours a week and involves three people copying data between systems. New client intake requires a staff member to manually pull details from emails and enter them into the project tool. Customer support tickets go unanswered for 48 hours because nobody is triaging volume overnight.

This specificity does two things. First, it immediately filters out consultants whose pitch is transformation-level abstraction — they will struggle to engage with a concrete operational problem because their entire model is built around selling a journey, not solving a workflow. Second, it gives you a measuring stick. Once the work is done, you will know whether it succeeded, because you named what success looks like before the engagement started.

Pick one workflow first. Resist the urge to hire someone for "general AI advice" — name the workflow, whether that is invoice processing, lead intake, or support triage, before you interview consultants.
 This one step, done before any sales calls, will filter the majority of mismatched providers immediately.

## Step Two: Look for Evidence of Shipped Work, Not Credentials

Once you have your problem defined and you are ready to evaluate candidates, resist the pull of the credential stack. The AI consulting market has no meaningful professional licensing body, which means certifications primarily signal that someone paid for a course. What actually matters is whether they have built something that real people use in production.

A small business AI consultant should earn their fee on delivery, not slideware. Before you hire, ask to see one workflow they automated end to end, the hours it saved per week, and how they kept a person in control of anything touching customer data.
 This is a more revealing test than any portfolio document, because it forces specificity. A consultant who built a real system can answer every follow-up question: who uses it, how often, what it replaced, what broke during testing, and what they would do differently next time. A consultant who has only advised on systems or demoed vendor platforms will give you vague, hedging answers.

Evaluate candidates on SMB-specific experience, relevant industry expertise, transparent and scoped pricing, and hands-on implementation capability rather than advisory alone. Request case studies from businesses similar in size and sector to yours. Start with a discovery call to assess fit — a good consultant will ask as many questions about your business as you ask about their services.

Pay attention to how specifically they answer every question. Specificity is the signal. Generality is the warning.

## Step Three: Ask Directly About Tool Independence

The AI tooling landscape is full of referral arrangements, platform partnerships, and reseller agreements. This matters to you because a consultant who earns a commission or referral fee when you buy a specific platform is not a neutral advisor — they are a salesperson with a consulting title. Their recommendation will optimize for their own economics, not for your workflow.

Software vendor services teams are best when you have already chosen the platform — but the risk is that they may optimize for their tool, not your full workflow.
 The same dynamic applies to independent consultants with undisclosed platform arrangements. The fix is simple: ask the question directly. "Do you receive any referral fees, commissions, or other compensation from any platform or tool you might recommend to us?" A consultant with nothing to hide will answer without hesitation. One with conflicts will deflect, reframe the question, or become defensive — all useful signals.

Tool independence also has a downstream consequence worth examining during the evaluation. Ask whether the systems they build will be documented well enough that another developer or internal employee could maintain them. 
Keep the code, prompts, configuration, and every model account registered to your business — because if the accounts are not yours, neither is the workflow.
 A consultant who insists on holding the keys after the engagement ends is building a maintenance dependency, not a solution.

## Step Four: Insist on a Fixed-Scope Pilot Before Any Larger Commitment

One of the clearest signals that a consultant is protecting their own hours rather than your budget is the inability — or unwillingness — to scope a starter engagement at a defined price. Open-ended time-and-materials arrangements with vague deliverables are where small business AI budgets disappear without measurable results.

Require a fixed-scope phase one. Time-and-materials engagements without a written scope create unbounded bills. A capable AI business consultant can scope and price the first phase before you sign.
 The pilot does not need to be enormous — the point is to produce something real that proves the approach works before you commit to anything larger. 
The right consultant scopes a single workflow, ships a working pilot, and shows that it works before widening the engagement.

What should a good scoped proposal look like? 
A useful proposal for small businesses should name the workflow, the systems involved, the expected time savings, the review process, and the first 30-day deliverable.
 If a proposal you receive is missing any of those elements, send it back and ask for them. If the consultant cannot add them, you have learned something important about how they will behave once the engagement starts.

Ask for the price of that first phase in writing. A consultant who will not attach a number to a defined deliverable is a risk regardless of their technical ability.

## Step Five: Know the Red Flags That Should End the Conversation

Some signals do not require further analysis. They are disqualifying, and recognizing them early saves significant time and money.

### They Lead With Technology Before Understanding Your Business

A bad consultant opens with: "You need to implement AI agents with multi-modal processing capabilities." A good consultant opens with: "Tell me about your biggest bottleneck right now — what's taking up the most time and creating the most friction? Let's see if AI can solve it."
 The order of those two conversations reveals everything about how an engagement will go.

### They Make Guaranteed Outcome Promises

No consultant can promise specific outcomes — an uplift in revenue, a specific cost reduction — without first understanding your business, your data, and your current operations. If someone leads with guarantees, they're selling, not consulting.

### There Is No Discovery Phase

A consultant who skips discovery — who sends you a proposal before spending meaningful time understanding your workflows, your team, your data, and your current systems — is templating an answer to a question they have not fully read. 
Business grounding is the first test: does this person ask more questions about your business than about your technology? A consultant who leads with "what tools are you using?" before understanding your margins, your team's capacity, or your actual bottlenecks is working backward. Good consultants start with the problem, not the solution.

### The System Only They Can Maintain

Lock-in is a business model disguised as a service. If the consultant builds systems that require their proprietary methods, their private API keys, or their ongoing involvement to function — and cannot hand off documentation that gives you real operational independence — you are not buying a solution. You are buying a dependency. 
Treat AI as ongoing, not one-and-done: AI tools and your business both change, so plan for periodic review — but that review should be possible without the original vendor.

### They Cannot Name a Specific Metric They Will Move

Disqualify any consultant who promises broad transformation without naming one workflow.
 Before the engagement begins, both parties should agree on what success looks like in measurable terms — hours saved per week, reduction in error rate, response time improvement. If the consultant resists that conversation, their business model depends on ambiguity.

## The Evaluation in Practice

Running through these steps — define your problem, demand evidence of shipped work, check for tool conflicts, insist on a fixed-scope pilot, and filter out the red flags — takes time, but far less time than recovering from a bad engagement. The process also makes the conversation cleaner when you do find the right match, because you arrive with a specific problem, a defined budget range, and a clear picture of what good looks like.

No Black Swan is built to pass every one of these tests. The work begins with your workflows — not with a product pitch or a technology recommendation — and every engagement is scoped before it is priced. If you are ready to start the evaluation process with a partner who will ask more questions about your business than your tools, a no-cost discovery call is the right first step. The call is structured around your operations, not a sales deck, and it produces a concrete picture of where AI can realistically help before any commitment is made.

## Key Takeaways

- Define your problem in operational terms — specific workflow, measurable time cost — before contacting any consultant; this single step filters out the majority of mismatched providers.
- Demand evidence of shipped work over certifications: ask to see a live system real employees use today, and pay close attention to how specifically the consultant answers every follow-up.
- Ask directly whether the consultant receives referral fees or commissions from any tool they might recommend — undisclosed platform relationships mean their advice optimizes for their economics, not your workflow.
- Insist on a fixed-scope, fixed-price pilot before any larger commitment; a consultant who cannot scope a starter engagement at a defined price is protecting their hours, not your budget.
- Walk away from any consultant who leads with technology before understanding your business, makes guaranteed outcome promises, skips a discovery phase, or builds systems only they can maintain.
