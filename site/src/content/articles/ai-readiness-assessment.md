## The Short Answer

An **AI readiness assessment** is a structured evaluation of whether and where a business can put AI to work. A useful one covers **five areas—workflows, systems, data, people, and risk**—and ends with a **ranked list of priorities**, not a maturity score. For a smaller or mid-sized business, the practical question is not whether the organization scores well on a capability index; it is whether there are identifiable workflows that would benefit, accessible systems, someone who can own the change, and a team that can adopt it. If your answer is yes across those areas for even one workflow, you likely have somewhere sensible to start.

## What Is an AI Readiness Assessment?

AI readiness is frequently overcomplicated. Businesses can spend months scoring themselves on data-maturity models, infrastructure tiers, and governance frameworks before anyone has asked the more useful question: does this company have a workflow that wastes real time, information that is reachable, and someone willing to change how the work gets done?

A practical AI readiness assessment is a structured look at whether and where a business can actually put AI to use. It covers how work currently flows, what systems and data exist and whether they are accessible, who has the authority to make decisions, and what the realistic risks of change are. A good assessment ends with a ranked list of what to do—and an honest view of what not to do. It does not end with a composite score and a congratulatory PDF.

This matters because the alternative is spending money on tools or projects that do not fit the business. The assessment is the diagnostic step that separates real opportunities from technically impressive but operationally useless interventions. The five areas worth examining are:

**Workflows.** Which processes repeat regularly and consume meaningful time? The question is not whether the task is complex, but whether it is predictable enough and high-volume enough to be worth improving. Manual data entry, routine customer follow-up, internal reporting, document review—these are starting points, not guarantees.

**Systems.** What tools and platforms does the business already use, and can they be connected? Many businesses can generate meaningful value by improving existing workflows and connecting the systems they already have. A complete technology overhaul is rarely a prerequisite.

**Data.** Is the information a workflow needs available, reasonably accurate, and accessible? Data access, quality, confidentiality, and security constraints must be understood before any implementation begins. The assessment needs to surface these constraints, not discover them mid-build.

**People.** Does someone have the authority to make decisions and own the change? Are the people doing the work involved in identifying opportunities? Is management prepared to change existing processes rather than simply add another tool on top of them? These are organizational questions, and they matter as much as technical ones.

**Risk.** What could go wrong, and how consequential would it be? Risk covers errors in AI-supported decisions, client confidentiality, regulatory constraints, and the operational impact if a new workflow underperforms. Some risks are manageable; others mean a workflow is not a good candidate right now.

An assessment that covers all five of these areas gives you an honest picture of what is worth attempting, what prerequisites need to be addressed first, and where AI is simply not the right answer. You do not need a perfect technology stack or immaculate data to start. Most businesses can find at least one workflow worth improving using tools and information they already have.

## How Do I Know If My Business Is Ready for AI?

The question of readiness is best answered not at the organization level, but at the workflow level. Rather than asking "Is our business ready for AI?", ask it about a specific, concrete piece of work: "Is this workflow ready for AI?"

Three conditions matter more than anything else. First, the workflow repeats at meaningful volume and costs real time—either owner time, manager time, or enough employee hours to justify change. Second, the information the workflow needs is reachable: it exists somewhere, it is accessible with appropriate permissions, and it is accurate enough to work with. Third, someone in the business has the authority and willingness to own the change—to make the decision, involve the team, and see it through adoption.

If those three conditions are met for even one workflow, the business has a viable starting point. Readiness is also not a permanent state: as systems improve, as the team builds experience with new tools, and as constraints change, workflows that were previously impractical can become genuinely viable. An honest assessment maps where you are now and identifies what would need to change to open up future opportunities.

What readiness is not: a function of how technologically sophisticated the organization is, how modern its stack is, or how many AI tools it has already purchased. A business running on spreadsheets and email can still have high-value, low-risk automation opportunities. A business that has bought half a dozen AI subscriptions may still have no coherent workflow to show for it.

## An AI Readiness Checklist for a Mid-Sized Business

A practical checklist for a 20-to-200-person business does not need to be long. The goal is to establish whether the minimum conditions for a sensible first implementation are in place, and to identify what needs to be resolved before work begins. Here is what to work through:

**One named owner with real authority.** Who is accountable for this initiative? Not a committee—a person. That person needs to be able to make decisions, involve the relevant team members, and commit time to adoption. Without a clear owner, even a well-designed implementation stalls.

**A shortlist of candidate workflows with rough volume and time-cost estimates.** What processes repeat regularly and take meaningful time? For each one, note approximately how often it runs, how long it takes, and how many people touch it. This does not need to be a formal process audit—a rough working list is enough to get started.

**Systems and access mapped.** What tools and platforms does the business use? Which systems would a candidate workflow touch? Do you have access to APIs, export functions, or integration points? Are there systems where access is restricted, and do you know why?

**Data and confidentiality constraints identified.** What information does each candidate workflow rely on? Where does that information live? Are there client-confidentiality obligations, regulatory constraints, or data-quality problems that would affect what AI can do with it? These constraints do not necessarily disqualify a workflow, but they shape what implementation looks like and must be known before anything is built.

**A baseline to measure against.** How does the workflow perform today? Time per task, error rate, volume handled, turnaround time—whatever the relevant metric is. Without a baseline, it is very difficult to know whether a change made things better, and by how much.

This checklist does not require an external consultant to complete. A thoughtful internal review can produce most of it. What it does require is honest answers: processes that look messier up close than they do from a distance, data that turns out to be less tidy than expected, and owners who are more time-constrained than they realized. That honesty is what makes the subsequent work useful.

## Public Frameworks vs. a Practical Assessment

Several well-regarded public governance frameworks exist for organizations that need to manage AI-related risk in a structured, auditable way. These frameworks organize work around governing, mapping, measuring, and managing AI risk across an organization. They are well-constructed, widely referenced, and genuinely useful—for the purposes they were designed for: compliance readiness, enterprise governance, and responsible AI development at scale. What they are not designed to do is tell a 50-person business which workflow to change first.

Public AI governance frameworks are built for policy, risk governance, and regulated environments. They assume a level of organizational maturity—cross-functional governance committees, documented AI inventories, formal risk management programs—that most smaller businesses simply do not have yet. That is not a criticism; it is a scope difference. Using an enterprise governance framework as a starting point for a practical workflow improvement is like using a city planning code to decide whether to rearrange your office furniture.

For a smaller business, the more useful frame is operational: start with the workflows, not the governance architecture. Identify what is worth improving, understand the constraints, assess the risks at the workflow level, and prioritize by business value. That is a faster, more grounded path to a useful first result than scoring organizational maturity across six dimensions.

That said, if your business operates in a regulated sector, handles sensitive personal data, or is subject to contractual AI governance requirements, the governance dimension moves up the priority list. Risk at the workflow level and regulatory risk at the organizational level are both real—the question is which one is immediately material to your situation.

## Who Can Do an AI Assessment of My Business?

There are three realistic options, each with a different tradeoff. **Do it yourself with a structured checklist.** This is a reasonable starting point for a business that wants to develop an internal view before engaging anyone externally. The risk is that the people closest to the work often find it hardest to see where it could be different. Manual processes that have been in place for years start to look inevitable. A self-assessment is better than nothing, but it tends to miss the opportunities that are obvious to anyone coming in from outside.

**A paid, independent assessment from someone without a product to sell.** This is the most commercially honest option. The assessor's only job is to understand your business and give you a useful, objective view of where AI can help and where it cannot. The output should be a clear, written summary: opportunities ranked by value, effort, and risk; constraints identified; and an honest list of where AI does not belong. The scope and fee should be agreed before work begins, and the deliverable should be clear.

**A free AI readiness audit from a vendor selling their own platform.** These can be useful as a starting point, and some are genuinely well-constructed. But the recommendations will naturally be shaped by what the vendor sells. If the vendor's product is an AI customer-service tool, their assessment is likely to surface customer-service workflow opportunities. That is not necessarily wrong—it may happen to match your situation—but it is not independent advice. Before accepting a vendor-led assessment as your basis for investment, ask what you will hold in your hands at the end, and whether the output would look different if the assessor had no product to follow up with.

The question to ask in every case is what the deliverable actually is. A score is not a plan. A maturity tier is not a prioritized workflow list. A good assessment ends with something you can act on: a ranked set of opportunities, a list of prerequisites, and an honest view of where the effort is not worth it.

## What Should It Take, and What Do You Get?

A practical AI readiness assessment for a smaller business is not a months-long engagement. It is a fixed-scope, fixed-fee piece of work with clearly defined deliverables, agreed before the work begins.

The right output is short and usable. It should include: a ranked list of candidate workflows with an assessment of business value, implementation effort, and associated risk; a summary of system and data constraints that affect what is feasible now; a view of organizational readiness—who can own this, what adoption looks like; and an honest list of where AI does not make sense, either because the opportunity is not material or because the prerequisites are not in place.

What the output should not be is a long strategy document full of frameworks and recommendations that require another six months to interpret. The business should be able to read it and immediately understand what to do first, what to leave alone for now, and what needs to be resolved before the next step.

The scope matters as much as the deliverable. An assessment that takes the right time for the business—not padded out to match a standard enterprise timeline, not compressed into a two-minute online quiz—gives you a genuinely calibrated view of your situation. For a mid-sized business with a handful of candidate workflows, a focused discovery engagement should be completable in weeks.

One more practical point: the assessment should tell you clearly where AI is not the answer. That is not a failure of the engagement. It is one of the most useful things the work can produce.

The most recent data makes the case for a more grounded approach to AI readiness assessment more plainly than ever. Kyndryl's third annual Readiness Report, released in November 2026, describes a "widening divide" between the returns organizations are seeing from AI and their actual preparedness to manage the risks and demands that come with expanded AI use—with average readiness for external risks falling to just 24%, down seven points from the prior year. Meanwhile, Thryv's 2026 AI and Small Business Adoption Report found that 66 percent of small businesses now use AI, up from 55 percent the previous year, suggesting that adoption is spreading faster than structured implementation thinking. Research published by Accenture in May 2026, drawing on executives from 2,000 companies across 15 countries, found that while 64 percent of respondents had moved advanced AI beyond pilots, only 7 percent had achieved the data readiness required to scale it. The pattern across all three sources is consistent: more businesses are using AI tools, fewer have done the foundational work to use them well, and the gap between adoption and readiness is widening rather than closing.
