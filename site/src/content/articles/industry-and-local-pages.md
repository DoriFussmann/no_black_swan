A general service page can explain an offer. It starts to fail when two readers need different examples before they can tell whether the offer applies to them. A law firm and a field-service company may both want less manual handoff work, and they may still not recognize themselves in the same paragraph. That is the practical reason to consider an industry page or a location page. It is a reason about the reader, not a reason to publish a page for every noun you can think of.

No Black Swan does not publish a catalog of industry or city pages. This article is about the decision, not a directory of pages on this site.

## Start with the question the page has to answer

Before writing a vertical or a city into the navigation, write down the question a buyer on that page is trying to answer. Usually it is one of these:

- Is this offer meant for a business like mine?
- Which constraints should I expect the first conversation to cover?
- What kind of workflow is a sensible starting point in this setting?

If the honest answers are the same as the answers already on the main service page, another URL will not help. It will split attention and create a page that has to be maintained. If the answers are genuinely different — different systems, different approval habits, different words for the same task — a separate page can earn its place.

## What belongs on the page

A useful industry page stays close to operations. It names the kinds of work that tend to create friction in that setting, and it says what the provider will not assume. It does not need a tour of every tool on the market. It does not need a claim that a particular integration already exists.

Location pages are similar, with one extra discipline. Geography sometimes changes the vendors, the filing habits, or the competitive set a buyer lives with. A page can talk about that context. It should not imply an office, a local staff, or a regulated presence that the business does not have. Remote work and local context are different facts. Say only the one that is true.

## What to leave off

Pages in this category go bad in a few repeatable ways.

They invent proof. A sentence that sounds like a case study, a percentage, or a client logo is not a substitute for an approved example. If you do not have permission and a real result, do not write one.

They promise a menu the site does not have. "See our page for your city" is a broken promise if the page is not there. "Pages for other industries are in development" tells the reader to wait for something that may never be published. Either publish the page or do not point at it.

They hide the actual offer. A visitor who already knows they need an assessment, an implementation, or help after launch should still be able to find those services in one step. A vertical page is a doorway, not a replacement for the service explanation.

## A simpler test

Use three checks before adding the URL.

1. The reader can tell, in the first screen, whether the page is for them.
2. At least one section would be wrong or misleading if you pasted it onto the general service page unchanged.
3. You can name who will update the page when the offer changes.

If a check fails, keep the material on the main service page or in the sales conversation. No Black Swan uses the discovery call for that specificity: the business, the workflow, and the constraints come from the client, not from a grid of city pages.

## Where this leaves a buyer

If you are choosing a consultant, treat a pile of near-duplicate city pages as a weak signal. Look instead for whether the firm can talk about your workflow without pretending it already knows your systems. The service pages on this site describe assessment, strategy, implementation, automation, and support after deployment. None of them depends on a hidden industry index.

If you are planning your own site, publish the specific page only when it answers a different question. Until then, one clear page outperforms a set of placeholders.

## Key Takeaways

- Add an industry or location page when the reader's question, constraints, or language are actually different.
- Do not imply offices, local teams, client results, or a library of pages you have not published.
- Keep a direct path to the real offer. A vertical page should not trap the reader away from the service.
