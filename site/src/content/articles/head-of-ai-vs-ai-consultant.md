## The Short Answer

**Head of AI vs. AI consultant** comes down to one question: is there already a full-time job's worth of AI work inside your business? **Hire a full-time head of AI** (fixed salary, months to onboard) when AI is central to your product or the implementation pipeline is already continuous. **Use an outside implementation partner** (a fee for defined scope, faster to start) when the opportunity is real but not yet a full-time mandate. **Many companies do the second to find out whether they need the first** — the engagement surfaces what the role would actually involve and makes the eventual hire far easier to get right.

## The Problem With Promoting Someone From Inside

The idea of taking a star performer from the existing team and making them "Head of AI" sounds efficient. It almost never is.

These are talented people doing important work. The moment you redirect them, something that was running well starts running worse. The department they were managing does not manage itself; the relationships they held do not transfer automatically; the institutional knowledge they carried does not get documented before they pivot. You end up with two problems: a function that is now under-managed and an AI program that is being led by someone who, however capable, is learning on the job while also paying an opportunity cost the business cannot easily see.

The same logic applies — and perhaps more forcefully — to the IT manager. This is a common suggestion, and it comes from a reasonable instinct: IT already deals with systems, so AI must be adjacent. The issue is not whether the IT manager has a relationship with AI tools; everyone does. The deeper issue is that IT departments are designed, quite deliberately, to be protocol-driven and reactive. They maintain stability, enforce standards, manage risk and respond to problems. That is exactly what you want from an IT function. What it does not describe is someone who will walk into operations, finance, sales and customer service, ask hard questions about how work actually gets done, challenge established workflows and push the business toward changes nobody has asked for yet. AI integration requires sustained initiative across the organization, and that sits outside the natural posture of an IT department.

This does not mean IT is excluded. IT needs to be in the room whenever AI decisions are being made, with a genuine voice on access, security, integrations and risk. Give IT a veto on risk. Do not give IT the whole agenda.

One more thing worth naming: a consultant or outside partner solves a genuinely difficult problem in hiring that most businesses have not anticipated. If your HR team is asked today to write a job description for a head of AI, the result will almost certainly be generic — something about implementing AI systems across the organization, driving adoption, working with stakeholders. It will name a project or two. What it will not do is describe what the role looks like inside your specific business: which workflows it owns, which decisions it makes, what a good outcome looks like in year one, and what experience would actually qualify someone to do it. Those answers require someone who has already spent time inside your operations. An outside partner, working through a real engagement, generates exactly that material. The job description that comes out of a live implementation is specific, credible and far more useful for attracting and assessing the right candidate. That is worth a great deal.

## Hire When the Work Is Full-Time. Partner When It Isn't.

The simplest version of this decision: hire when the work is genuinely continuous and full-time; use a partner when it is real but not yet a permanent job. What makes AI work full-time? A continuous backlog of initiatives across the business; a need to maintain and evolve systems already in production; a strategic role that requires deep institutional knowledge accumulated over time; and a scope that one person outside the company cannot reasonably cover on a shared schedule. If AI is core to the product — not just improving operations around the product, but constituting what the product does — a permanent internal hire probably belongs in that architecture from the start.

For most other businesses, that point has not been reached. The work is real, the opportunity is genuine, and nothing will happen unless someone takes responsibility for it. But the pipeline is not full-time, the scope is not yet clear, and hiring a senior AI leader before understanding what the role involves is an expensive way to find out. Using an outside partner first is not a compromise; it is a more rational sequence. The partner does the diagnostic and implementation work, the business learns what AI can actually do inside its specific operations, and by the end of that engagement the case for a permanent hire — or the absence of one — is far better grounded.

## What Each Option Actually Costs

The cost comparison between a full-time head of AI and an outside partner is rarely done honestly, because the full cost of a hire is easy to undercount. According to Pearson Carter's 2026 Data & AI Salary Guide, Heads of AI sit at a median of $259,000 in total compensation in the United States. That is base salary before benefits, employer taxes, recruiting fees and the management attention required to hire well and onboard effectively. Filling a senior AI role takes an average of 114 days, compared with 52 days for a general backend engineer — and the gap widens every quarter. Even once the person starts, there is a ramp period — typically measured in months — before they have understood the business well enough to have a meaningful impact. A senior hire can easily consume six to nine months before it delivers something the business could not have done without it.

An outside partner costs a fee and some management attention. The scope is agreed before work begins, the engagement starts quickly, and the work is happening from the first week rather than the third month. The owner's time still matters — the partner needs access to the business, its people and its systems — but that cost is bounded and predictable in a way that a hiring process is not.

Count both directions honestly. If the business is paying for a full-time head of AI while the work is still being scoped, the hire is a fixed cost in search of a project. If the business is using an outside partner while the pipeline of AI work is already clearly full-time, it may be under-resourcing a function that justifies a permanent seat. Neither extreme is rational; the question is which one describes your situation today.

## Scale-Ups and Mid-Sized Companies: In-House or Outsourced?

The right answer differs by company type, and the two most common situations pull in different directions. Scale-ups with engineering teams often already have people who can build things. What they frequently lack is someone who decides what is worth building. Engineers build what they are told. If nobody inside the organization has the operational perspective and commercial judgment to identify which AI initiatives will actually change how the business performs, the engineering capacity is not the bottleneck. Direction is. An outside partner with operator experience can fill that gap, working alongside the engineering team to identify and prioritize the right work, and stepping back once the pipeline and architecture are clear.

Mid-sized companies without engineering teams face a different problem. They need someone who can build, not just recommend. Outsourcing implementation works here when one person inside the business owns the outcome — not running every technical decision, but holding the partner accountable, understanding what is being built and making sure it gets used. "Mid-market companies are actually outsourcing more," according to recent reporting, "recognizing that it's not just a labor arbitrage play but a way to access talent and thought leadership they couldn't build internally" — especially given how difficult it is to hire AI and technical talent right now.

The pattern that works across both types: internal ownership of the agenda, external delivery of execution. The business decides what matters and holds the partner accountable. The partner does the diagnostic, design and implementation work. That is not a compromise between hiring and outsourcing; it is a deliberate structure that gets more done, faster, while keeping commercial control inside the business.

## AI Engineer vs. Outside Partner: Who Decides What Gets Built?

This is the question that tends to get skipped, and skipping it is expensive. An AI engineer, or a narrow technical specialist hired to build automations, will build what they are asked to build. They are good at it. The assumption embedded in that arrangement is that someone inside the business already knows what is worth building, in which order, and how the pieces connect to each other and to the existing operating model. That assumption is usually wrong.

Most businesses know roughly which parts of their operation feel painful. What they do not have is a systematic view of which interventions would change the economics most, what the dependencies are, what the technical risk of each option is, and what the simplest effective solution looks like rather than the most technically impressive one. Without that view, an engineer builds what gets pointed at, which is not always the highest-value thing.

A specialist hire is also difficult to assess. If nobody inside the business can judge the quality of the work, it is hard to know whether the hire is performing well, until something breaks or the deliverables fail to change anything. This is a real risk with narrow technical hires in a domain where the evaluator and the evaluated are working from asymmetric information.

An outside partner who also implements — not just advises — covers both problems for a period. The diagnostic work establishes what should be built and why. The implementation work produces results against a clear brief. And the whole engagement happens under a commercial relationship where scope and fees are agreed in advance, which keeps the relationship honest.

Should a startup hire an AI engineer or bring in an outside partner? For a startup where AI is not the product, an outside partner is almost always the better first move. It is faster, it is cheaper than a senior hire, and it generates the material the startup needs to eventually make a good full-time decision — if the work turns out to justify one.

## Should My IT Manager Run the AI Agenda?

IT is essential. IT is not sufficient.

The systems that AI touches are IT's territory: access controls, integrations, data flows, security posture, vendor relationships and the technical risk that comes with connecting new tools to live business systems. Any serious AI implementation that bypasses IT creates problems — sometimes immediately, sometimes quietly over months. IT needs a genuine role, not a courtesy consultation.

But the AI agenda itself — deciding which workflows to change, which manual processes to automate, which information bottlenecks to fix, which customer interactions to accelerate — is an operations and commercial call. It requires someone who understands how the business makes money, how the team actually works, and where the friction costs the most. IT departments are built to maintain what exists and protect against risk. That is valuable, and it is a different capability from driving change across operations, sales, customer service and finance.

Give IT a veto on risk. Bring IT into every technical decision. Do not hand IT the AI agenda and expect initiative and commercial judgment to emerge from a function whose job is to be careful and reactive. The business will get careful, reactive AI outcomes, which is a polite way of saying very little will change.

## What Should a Services Firm Do?

Services firms — law firms, accountancies, consultancies, agencies, managed service providers — have a specific version of this problem, shaped by two constraints that do not apply the same way in product or operational businesses.

The first constraint is billable hours. Every hour a fee earner spends on internal AI projects is an hour not billed to a client. Pulling a good lawyer, accountant or consultant off client work to run an AI initiative is a decision with a direct and measurable cost. It is rarely the right decision, even when the person is well-placed to understand the work.

The second constraint is client confidentiality. Client data flowing through AI systems — whether for document drafting, matter research, financial modeling or reporting — raises questions that cannot be answered carelessly. Governance has to be right before client-facing work begins, not after.

The practical implication is sequencing. Start with internal administrative and delivery workflows that do not touch client data: knowledge management, proposal drafting, internal reporting, scheduling, billing administration. These are lower risk, faster to implement and high value in a business where professional time is the most expensive input. An outside partner can move quickly on this work without requiring a fee earner to step back from client responsibilities.

Client-facing AI is a later-stage question. When the internal foundations are working and the governance posture is clear, the conversation about how AI can support delivery — research, drafting, analysis, client reporting — becomes much more concrete and much less risky. For now, the first job is making the internal operation more efficient so that fee earners spend more time on billable work, not less.
