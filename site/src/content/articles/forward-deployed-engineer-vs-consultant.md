## The Short Answer

A **[forward deployed engineer](/articles/forward-deployed-engineer/)** (someone who embeds inside your operation and builds there) and a **consultant** (someone who studies your problem and recommends a solution) are measured by different things: one by what runs, the other by what is decided. If you already know which workflow needs to change and want it built into your systems, the engineering way of working is what you need. If you do not know where to start—which is most smaller businesses—you need diagnosis first, and ideally the same person who diagnoses also implements. For businesses that do not need a full-time hire, the practical question is not which title to look for, but which working model to insist on.

## What Each One Leaves Behind

The distinction between a consultant and a forward deployed engineer is fundamentally about proximity to the business and responsibility for execution. A consultant typically studies the problem, prepares a recommendation, and moves on. A forward deployed engineer works inside the operation, builds the solution, and stays accountable for the result after launch. One is measured by what is decided; the other by what runs.

That gap between deciding and doing is where most AI projects quietly die. Businesses receive recommendations, but then have to find the internal resources, technical expertise, and organizational capacity to implement them. Nobody steps up to own that translation, the team is already busy, and the recommendations age in a folder. The traditional consulting model creates this gap by design: the deliverable is advice, not a working system.

A forward deployed approach collapses that gap. Rather than receiving a fully formed specification and disappearing to build something, the operator spends time understanding the real workflow and its constraints—the actual sequence of steps, the people involved, the edge cases that never make it into a brief, and the systems that hold everything together. That understanding is what makes the eventual solution fit rather than merely work in a demo.

This matters most for smaller and mid-sized businesses, where processes are often undocumented, responsibilities overlap between people, and nobody has the time to translate operational problems into technical requirements. When workflows live in people's heads and in informal habits built up over years, a consultant cannot surface them through interviews alone. The engineer who watches the work happen—and asks questions from inside the process—finds a different and more useful picture.

The most effective model for many smaller businesses is therefore one person, or one accountable partner, who can diagnose, prioritize, design, build, integrate, and refine. Splitting those responsibilities across multiple providers creates handoffs, and handoffs are where things fall through. The client ends up coordinating everything—the consultant, the developer, the automation specialist—while nobody owns the whole outcome.

The practical implication is straightforward: technical ability without operational understanding produces solutions that do not fit the business. Business consulting without implementation ability produces another set of recommendations the business has to execute alone. The person doing the work needs to understand both. And the value of that combination is not simply having someone build software. It is having someone who can recognize which problems are worth solving, understand the implications of changing a process, and take responsibility for making the solution work in real use.

## Which Do You Actually Need?

**Forward deployed engineer vs AI consultant: which do I need?** The honest answer depends on what you already know. If you can describe the workflow in reasonable detail and your problem is that nobody is building anything, you need implementation: the engineering way of working. If you are not sure which parts of your operation are worth improving, or in what order, you need diagnosis before you need a builder.

The complication is that for most businesses without a large internal team, those two needs appear together. The business does not know where to start, so it hires a consultant to find out. The consultant delivers a roadmap. Then the business has to find someone else to build it—and that someone has no context, no relationship with the team, and no accountability for the outcome the original recommendations pointed toward.

The cleaner arrangement is one accountable person who can do both: spend time inside the business to understand what needs to change, then build it. That is not a standard consulting engagement, and it is not a standard development project. It is a working model—and the working model is what you should evaluate, not the title on the proposal.

Start with a defined workflow and a measurable outcome. Agree what needs to change, who owns it, what will be delivered, and how you will know it is working. If the person you are evaluating cannot describe what they will build and by when, they are selling advice, not execution.

## FDE vs Head of AI for a Scale-Up

**Forward deployed engineer or head of AI for a scale-up?** These are different functions at different stages. A head of AI sets strategic direction, defines standards, hires a team, and builds a function over time. A forward deployed engineer—or someone working in that way—ships one workflow after another, in sequence, inside the business as it currently operates.

Scale-ups short on delivery do not need the first before they have the second. If your business has not yet built meaningful AI-enabled workflows, hiring a head of AI to set strategy for work that is not yet happening is premature. What creates value is the delivery itself: automating a real process, connecting a real system, replacing a real manual step. Once there are working systems to manage, a function to coordinate, and a team to lead, the head of AI role becomes useful.

A scale-up founder who is spending time coordinating between a consultant's recommendations and a freelance developer's execution has the wrong structure. The forward deployed working model—one person accountable from diagnosis through implementation and refinement—is what delivers progress. Direction-setting can follow once there is something to direct.

## FDE vs AI Automation Agency for a Services Firm

**FDE vs AI automation agency for a services firm?** An agency builds to a specification you give it. The embedded engineer finds the specification by watching the work. For services firms, that distinction is decisive.

Services businesses—professional services, advisory, managed services, healthcare operations, logistics—typically run on undocumented workflows, informal handoffs, and institutional knowledge held by individuals. The right automation for a client onboarding process, a quoting workflow, or an internal knowledge retrieval problem is almost never obvious until someone has watched how the work actually happens. What looks like a simple data transfer often involves several informal decision points that nobody has written down. What looks like a knowledge problem is often a coordination problem.

An agency working from a brief will build what the brief describes. If the brief is wrong or incomplete—which it usually is in a services context—the result will not fit. The embedded approach finds the real specification through proximity: sitting with the team, understanding the exceptions, and designing around the actual constraints. That cannot happen at a distance.

## Why the Title Is Rare Outside Large Software Companies

The forward deployed engineer role was created by enterprise software vendors—most prominently Palantir—to deploy their own complex products inside large organizations. The model was built for a specific context: regulated industries, significant security requirements, and the need for weeks of work before any software could be shown to a customer.

The AI wave has dramatically expanded the title. Data from labor market analytics firm Lightcast, reported in October 2026, counted FDE job postings exceeding 7,300 in the first nine months of 2026, up from fewer than 200 in 2023. Betts Recruiting found that Forward Deployed Engineer job postings rose more than 1,000% between January and August 2026 compared to the same period in 2025. The role has gone from a Palantir-specific model to something companies across the market are actively hiring.

But smaller businesses will rarely encounter the title in a form they can buy. The FDE role, as it exists at the frontier labs and large software vendors, comes attached to enterprise contracts and enterprise pricing. It is not structured for a fifty-person operation trying to improve a quoting process or a reporting workflow.

The same function—embedded, accountable, building in place—exists under different names and in different commercial arrangements. What matters is not whether someone calls themselves a forward deployed engineer, an AI consultant, an automation specialist, or an implementation partner. What matters is what they actually do and what they are accountable for delivering. Ask whether they will spend time inside your business before proposing anything. Ask what they will build, and by when, and whether they will own the outcome after launch. The working model answers the question the title cannot.

## How to Buy This Way of Working Without Hiring

You do not need to hire a full-time engineer to access this working model. What you need is a commercial arrangement that creates the right accountability without long-term lock-in.

Look for someone who will spend time inside the business before proposing anything. If the first conversation produces a proposal with specific tools and solutions already selected, the person is selling a product, not diagnosing your business. The first meaningful work should be understanding how the operation works—the workflows, the systems, the constraints, and where the friction actually sits.

Agree on one workflow and a specific outcome before anything is built. Not a broad transformation programme, not a list of twenty opportunities to pursue over two years. One thing that will visibly improve, with a date and a clear measure of whether it is working. That scope limits your risk and makes it possible to evaluate the result.

Keep the engagement month to month if you can. A flat monthly fee with no long-term commitment means the person doing the work earns continued access by delivering continued value. It also means you can stop if the working relationship is not productive, without unwinding a complicated contract. That structure concentrates accountability in the right place.

## Key Takeaways

- A forward deployed engineer works inside your operation and leaves behind a running system; a consultant studies the problem and leaves behind a recommendation. Most businesses need both from the same person.
- The working model matters more than the title. Ask what the person will build, what they are accountable for, and what happens after launch—not what they call themselves.
- For scale-ups, delivery gaps are more urgent than strategy gaps. A forward deployed working model ships real workflows; a head of AI function manages them once there are enough to manage.
- For services firms, the specification for any automation almost never exists before someone has spent time inside the actual work. An agency builds to a brief; an embedded operator finds the brief by watching.
- The right commercial arrangement is month to month, starts with time inside the business, and agrees one workflow and outcome before anything is built.
